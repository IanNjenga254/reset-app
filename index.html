Cloudflare Family DNS (1.1.1.3 / 1.0.0.3)

Blocks adult content at the DNS level across every app on a device. Free, no account needed for basic setup.

Router (whole-home)
1Log into your router's admin page (often 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1).
2Find the DNS settings, usually under WAN or Internet setup.
3Set primary DNS to 1.1.1.3 and secondary to 1.0.0.3.
4Save and reboot the router so all connected devices pick it up.
Windows
1Settings → Network & Internet → your connection → Edit DNS settings.
2Set to Manual, enable IPv4, enter 1.1.1.3 and 1.0.0.3.
macOS
1System Settings → Network → your connection → Details → DNS.
2Add 1.1.1.3 and 1.0.0.3, remove other DNS entries, apply.
iOS
1Install the "1.1.1.1 for Families" app — covers cellular data too, which a Wi-Fi-only DNS change won't.
2Choose the "Malware and Adult Content" filter option during setup.
Android
1Install "1.1.1.1 for Families", or set Private DNS manually to family.cloudflare-dns.com under Network settings.
2Confirm the app is set to the adult-content-filtering mode.
No filter is 100% foolproof. Pairing a technical block with a real person you're accountable to works better than either alone.

Second layer: app & device blockers

DNS alone can be bypassed with a VPN — a device-level blocker adds a harder-to-remove layer.

Cold Turkey Blocker (Windows)
1Create a blocking list of sites and apps to restrict.
2Use a "locked block" so it can't be turned off mid-session.
3Use Frozen Turkey to add a mandatory delay (hours or days) before any change to your settings takes effect — this is the key feature for urge moments.
4Set a schedule so blocks are always active during your highest-risk hours.
Covenant Eyes
•Pairs blocking with optional accountability reports sent to a person you choose — useful if you want a human in the loop, not just software.
BlockSite
•Cross-platform (desktop + mobile), supports scheduled blocking and a password lock on settings.
Freedom
•Syncs blocks across all your devices at once from one schedule.
Password-protect the blocker itself, using a password you don't have memorized (have someone else set it, or use a random generated one you don't save anywhere handy) — so it can't be disabled mid-urge.

Free / no-install option

A lighter layer if you want something quick with no software install.

•Browser extensions like uBlock Origin or LeechBlock can block specific sites per-browser — free, but only covers that one browser.
•A hosts-file edit (redirecting specific domains to 127.0.0.1) works system-wide without installing anything, though it takes a bit more technical comfort to set up and maintain.
These are easier to bypass than a locked blocker or DNS change — treat them as a starting layer, not the whole system.

Finding an accountability partner

Someone who knows what you're working on and checks in — pairing tech blocks with a real person is consistently the strongest combination.

1Pick someone you trust who won't shame you — a close friend, partner, or a support community.
2Be specific about what you're asking for: regular check-ins, honesty when asked, no judgment.
3Agree on how often you'll check in and what you'll actually share.

Cue → Craving → Response → Reward

Compulsive habits run on a loop, not a character flaw.

1Cue — a trigger: boredom, stress, a time of day, a place, being alone.
2Craving — the motivational pull that follows the cue; this is what actually drives behavior, not the cue itself.
3Response — the habitual action, in this case the compulsive behavior.
4Reward — brief relief or pleasure, which teaches the brain to repeat the loop next time the cue shows up.

Breaking the loop usually means interrupting it earlier — at the cue or craving stage — rather than trying to resist at the last second, which is the hardest point to stop.

Dopamine, tolerance, and escalation

A plain explanation, no moralizing.

Dopamine isn't a "pleasure chemical" so much as a signal that drives wanting and motivation. Repeated high-frequency stimulation can shift baseline sensitivity, so the same input produces less response over time — this is tolerance, the same mechanism behind escalation in other compulsive behaviors.

That's why compulsive use often trends toward more novelty, more frequency, or more extreme material over time — not a personal failing, but a predictable response of the reward system to repeated over-stimulation.

The encouraging side: this system is adaptable in both directions. Time away tends to allow baseline sensitivity to recover, which is part of why many people report other everyday pleasures feeling more vivid again after an extended break.

Myths & FAQ

Is this an "addiction" like substance use?
Experts differ on the exact classification, and it isn't officially listed as an addiction in major diagnostic manuals. What's well established is that compulsive patterns can form around this behavior the same way they can around other high-reward habits, and the same behavior-change tools tend to help regardless of the label.
Does one slip mean starting over from zero?
Only in the sense of a streak counter. The skills, insight, and progress you built don't disappear — that's a large part of why this app logs slips as data rather than as failure.
Will quitting fix everything else?
For most people it's one piece of a broader picture, not a cure-all. Sleep, stress, relationships, and other habits all interact with this one.
Is willpower alone enough?
Willpower helps in the moment but tends to be unreliable as a sole long-term strategy. Environment design (blockers), replacement behaviors, and support from other people generally outperform willpower alone.
